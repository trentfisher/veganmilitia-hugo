{{< img-thumb src="card.jpg" alt="one of my Technocracy, Inc. membership cards">}} Some years ago I was chatting with a friend and he made some reference to someone being a "technocrat".  He didn't expect my burst of laughter.  For all the times that word is used it is rarely used in the presense of someone who used to be a card-carrying technocrat.  Literally!
 
I have written [previously]({{<relref "2015-03-30-the-lessons-of-technocracy.markdown">}}) about the organization, but, lately, it has come up several times in relation to [Elon Musk's grandfather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_N._Haldeman), who was involved with the organization before World War II.  And the things said about the organization and their ideas were often a bit skewed, at best, and downright laughable in some cases.

Specifically, there are two places it has come up which has bugged me: the first was in Jill Lepore's podcast about Elon Musk, and the second is Gil Duran's book The Nerd Reich.  Both seem to play up the weirdest parts of the movement, and ignore some fundamental facts in order to ramrod it into their "techno-fascist" narrative.  Both of them seem to depend on a few secondary sources, and, apparently, they never even read the wikipedia page (or they cherry-picked it for things fitting their intended narrative).  This is a level of slopiness that I would not expect of those two.

It feels odd to defend an organization I dropped out of decades ago, but here we are.

Let's start at the beginning.  In the early 1930s, in the depths of the Great Depression, you had the paradoxical situation where there was nothing physically wrong with the farms (setting aside the Dust Bowl), factories, power stations, etc.  They could have produced everything everybody needed to live with plenty left over.  It was only the financial system which had collapsed.  The shared fiction of money had fallen apart.   It is easy to conclude that the money was the actual problem.  Technocrats took to calling this the "price system" to make it clear that both capitalism and communism are both vulnerable in the same ways.   From there they proposed measuring everything using energy, an objective measurement which cannot be manipulated for personal benefit by con-men on Wall Street.

The other phenomenon that was going on was the replacement of human labor with machines.  So fewer people were needed despite the fact that an "abundance" was being produced.  But rather than liberating people from work, it left the few left working squeezed tighter and tighter, while the rest begged for scraps.  The Technocrat's answer to this was to reduce the amount each person worked commensurately and ensure everyone had a job.  They had proposed that people work 4 hours a day, 4 days for 20 years, which doesn't sound bad.  Then everyone would get an equal share of what was produced.

They further proposed that those who know how to actually do things should be running the show:  technicians, engineers, etc. should actually run things.  Now someone who does not comprehend nuance would get tripped up here and think this is no different than Silicon Valley tech bros running things to enrich themselves.  The Technocrats stated that the entire goal was to run everything for the benefit of all, with everyone getting an **equal share** of the industrial output, of the "abundance" we are capable of producing.

Of course, this is all wildly simplistic and idealistic.  But, I think we can all agree that for technical tasks, you want someone qualified in that field managing it.  You want a farmer deciding how to plant a crop, you want a doctor deciding what treatment is needed.  But there is a level of policy and high-level direction which the Technocrats naively left out.  Let's use the metaphor of driving somewhere in a car.  You need two things, to decide where you are going, and actually operate the vehicle.  You cannot do one without the other, with no direction (policy) you'll get nowwhere or end up lost; and without a qualified operator, you'll get nowhere or end up in a crash.  So you still need a mechanism for setting policy, and, as Churchill said Democracy is the worst form of government aside from all the other ones we have tried.  Sadly Technocracy never realized that.

The fact that Elon Musk's grandfather was involved in the organization is really irrelevant.  He dropped out of the organization after a few years, probably because he realized it didn't line up with his racist, autocratic views.  And certainly, if Elon actually read and was able to understand what the Technocrats were saying, he would be vehemently opposed to them.  As would every single silicon valley techbro, every venture capitalist, every last one.

So I am willing to stick up for the ideals of Technocracy (though they are hopelessly naive and idealistic), but the organization itself, not so much.  I can only speak to what the organization was while I was a member (1983-2000) and what I have read about earier times.  I have no idea what it is like since then.
